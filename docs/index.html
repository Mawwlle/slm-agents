Summary

A recent position paper argues that agentic systems should run on small language models by default and call large models only when needed. It reports no experiment. We test the claim on four agentic tracks with 19 open models and 221,493 logged episodes, in four stages, each pre-registered before its first episode. Every decision rule was also run on label-shuffled data, and a rule that passed more than 5% of shuffled copies did not count.

  • A 4B model is a cheap substitute. Qwen3.5-4B solves 66% of the multi-turn tasks a 27B model solves, at a 7.4× lower cost per task. Below about 1.5B active parameters this falls apart.
  • Harness fixes are model-specific. Of two findings from twelve models, the one about instructions replicated on seven new models; the one about worked examples did not.
  • A large planner levels small executors, and costs nearly everything. A 350M model goes from 0% to 43% of multi-turn tasks, but the planner is 96% or more of the compute.
  • Prompted small planners don't hold the role. A 2.6B planner breaks the protocol in 91% of its turns. A router that plans only after a failure beats planning always, but a gain over the best single model is not shown.

What we tested

Three ways to make a small model pay in an agentic loop, each on the same tasks, models and harness.

Escalation

small modelfails →large model

A detector watches the first steps of a small model's episode and hands a likely failure to a large model.

Harness routing

model+its scaffold

Each model gets the scaffold that suits it: worked examples, instructions, a watchdog, constrained decoding.

Delegation

plannerstep ⇄ reportexecutor

A planner writes one step at a time and sees each result; a small executor turns the step into tool calls.

Cost is counted in FLOPs, summed over every call of an episode, planner and executor alike:

FLOPs = 2 · Nactive · (tprompt + tcompletion)

Quality is the pass rate, averaged over tracks. Differences are paired by task, with a 95% bootstrap interval and a permutation p. Any choice a rule makes, a threshold, a harness or a router, is made on fit tasks and scored on held-out ones.

Small models replace large ones, up to a point

For each model, the share of the test tasks solved by the 27B model (qwen3.8) that the model also solves alone. What matters is active parameters: Qwen3-VL-30B, a mixture of experts with 3B active, sits with the 3–4B models, at a 9.9× lower cost.

Any comparison of small models is first a comparison of the parsers that read them. Once a model's call shape is read, a more lenient parser mostly changes the count of broken steps, not the pass rate.

Stage 2 found two patterns on twelve models. Phase L tested both on seven models that had not been used to find them:

  • replicated Instructions do not help small models. The new small models' mean lift is −1.1 pp, against +6.0 pp for the three large ones.
  • not replicated Worked examples help small models. They helped 2 of the 7, against the pre-registered 5.

Delegation levels executors, at a price

qwen3.8 plans with thinking off: it sees the task, the tools and a report of every step, and writes one step at a time. The executor sees only that step and the tools it names. From 1.5B active parameters up, every executor reaches 55–59% of multi-turn tasks, whatever it solves alone. The plan is a ceiling as well as a floor.

Feedback is what makes it work. Against a planner that writes the whole plan at once and never sees results, seeing each report adds +40.2 pp for LFM2.5-1.2B and +57.7 pp for Qwen3.5-4B. But the planner is 96% or more of the compute of every delegated episode, so at that cost a single mid-sized model solves as much or more.

Cheap planners and a router

Stage 4 asks whether delegation pays with a cheaper planner, on 650 multi-turn and shell tasks. A prompted small planner first has to stay a planner: write a valid step, not call the tools itself.

A cheap 9B plan lifts the 350M executor by +20.4 pp but costs Qwen3.5-4B −15.9 pp, at 2.5× its cost. A router fixes the second half: it keeps the strong model alone and plans only where it is likely to fail. Drag the threshold.

Against the best single model at the same cost, the best router budget gains +3.1 pp, but the rule that may pick that budget out of 16 passed 14% of shuffled copies, so the gain is not shown. Prompted small planners do not pay; the systems where small planners work fine-tune them.

Calibration

A bootstrap interval protects one comparison. A rule that picks the best of many thresholds, harnesses or budgets, and then tests it, can pass on noise. So we ran every superiority rule on 50 copies of the data in which, per task, the labels of the compared systems are swapped at random. When the rule passes more than 5% of those copies, the verdict does not count.
